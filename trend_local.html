Each point rests on 14–27 labelled books. Other covers books with no single base tense and books that turned out not to be prose fiction — Amanda Gorman’s verse collections reached the hardcover fiction list and sit here rather than being dropped.

How a book gets its label

Each quote is sorted into one bucket. Only two of them carry evidence about the narrator’s tense — the other three exist to keep those two clean.

BucketWhat it isYields
event A specific character at a specific story-moment, or past-tense exposition about the story worldtense
dialogue Character speech — but the narrator’s own tag or action beat around it still countsbeat tense
gnomic Present-tense generalization reaching beyond the story world: proverbs, epigraphs, essayistic asides—
paratext Acknowledgments, dedications, jacket copy — not the novel —
unclear Undecidable from the excerpt alone—

The load-bearing split is gnomic against event, and the test is specificity, not tense. “My father is dead” is present tense but concerns one person at one moment, so it counts. “Books, like people, die” does not.

Those two evidence-bearing buckets pool into a single tally, and the book’s label follows in three steps:

  1. Gate. Fewer than five tense-bearing quotes and the book abstains rather than guessing.
  2. Base tense. A holistic read of the narrating situation — recorded independently of the counts — sets it: retrospective gives past, simultaneous gives present, dual gives other unless the tally runs 80% one way, which marks a base tense with a strand rather than true duality.
  3. Confidence. The tally then either corroborates that read or contradicts it, flagging the book for review.

The counts do not set the label because they cannot. Golden Girl and Cloud Cuckoo Land sit one percentage point apart and are structurally opposite — one alternates tense across its main narrative, the other is present-narrated with a past tale embedded inside it. No threshold separates those two; reading the narrating situation does.

What this does and doesn’t support

Within 2016–2025 the rise is statistically detectable — a linear trend across those ten years gives p = 0.007 on 229 books. The two-era split reads p = 0.004, but its 2019/2020 boundary was drawn after seeing the data, so lead with the trend.

The 1996–2015 points are context, not the same measurement. They come from the top 5 titles of each year against the top 30 from 2016 on, so the early slice is a smaller, more famous set of books. A difference between the eras could be a difference between those slices. Do not read the full span as one series.

Detectable is not monotonic. Adjacent years swing by as much as 23%, and 2018 (26%) sits at or above 2022 (25%), 2024 (26%). What the recent data supports is a level shift, not a steady year-on-year climb.

  • Goodreads pull-quotes are a biased source, and the bias runs toward apparent present tense. Everything below is a symptom of reading books through the passages readers chose to excerpt:
    • Quotes are selected for quotability, and quotability loves aphorism — 22% of all quotes are gnomic, timeless-present generalization that says nothing about a book’s narration. The bucket scheme exists to strip exactly this.
    • Quotation marks survive only 6–22% of the time, so speech and narration often cannot be told apart from the text alone.
    • Excerpts arrive clipped mid-sentence, stripping the attribution that would settle who is speaking.
    • How many quotes a book has tracks its fame, not its prose, so thinly-quoted books abstain more often — 18 do here.
    • Nothing marks where in a book a quote came from, so a framed prologue or an embedded tale reads the same as the main narration.
  • Quote-level labels are noisier than book-level ones. Scored against the project’s gold standard, two independent passes agreed on only 68% of quote buckets — yet both produced the same book label, and every disagreement was a bucket, never a tense. That measurement predates the current spec and is due a re-run.
